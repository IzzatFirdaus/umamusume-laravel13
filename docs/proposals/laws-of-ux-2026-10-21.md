# The Laws of UX

Status: **settled reference.** The Laws of UX as lawsofux.com states them, and how they bind the screens
in this repository.

Read from the source on **2026-10-21**. The statements are quoted, because a paraphrase of a law is a
different law.

## The twenty-nine, verbatim

### 1. Aesthetic-Usability Effect
> Users often perceive aesthetically pleasing design as design that's more usable.

### 2. Choice Overload
> The tendency for people to get overwhelmed when they are presented with a large number of options, often used interchangeably with the term paradox of choice.

### 3. Chunking
> A process by which individual pieces of an information set are broken down and then grouped together in a meaningful whole.

### 4. Cognitive Bias
> A systematic error of thinking or rationality in judgment that influence our perception of the world and our decision-making ability.

### 5. Cognitive Load
> The amount of mental resources needed to understand and interact with an interface.

### 6. Doherty Threshold
> Productivity soars when a computer and its users interact at a pace (<400ms) that ensures that neither has to wait on the other.

### 7. Fitts's Law
> The time to acquire a target is a function of the distance to and size of the target.

### 8. Flow
> The mental state in which a person performing some activity is fully immersed in a feeling of energized focus, full involvement, and enjoyment in the process of the activity.

### 9. Goal-Gradient Effect
> The tendency to approach a goal increases with proximity to the goal.

### 10. Hick's Law
> The time it takes to make a decision increases with the number and complexity of choices.

### 11. Jakob's Law
> Users spend most of their time on other sites. This means that users prefer your site to work the same way as all the other sites they already know.

### 12. Law of Common Region
> Elements tend to be perceived into groups if they are sharing an area with a clearly defined boundary.

### 13. Law of Proximity
> Objects that are near, or proximate to each other, tend to be grouped together.

### 14. Law of Prägnanz
> People will perceive and interpret ambiguous or complex images as the simplest form possible, because it is the interpretation that requires the least cognitive effort of us.

### 15. Law of Similarity
> The human eye tends to perceive similar elements as a complete picture, shape, or group, even if those elements are separated.

### 16. Law of Uniform Connectedness
> Elements that are visually connected are perceived as more related than elements with no connection.

### 17. Mental Model
> A compressed model based on what we think we know about a system and how it works.

### 18. Miller's Law
> The average person can only keep 7 (plus or minus 2) items in their working memory.

### 19. Occam's Razor
> Among competing hypotheses that predict equally well, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected.

### 20. Paradox of the Active User
> Users never read manuals but start using the software immediately.

### 21. Pareto Principle
> The Pareto principle states that, for many events, roughly 80% of the effects come from 20% of the causes.

### 22. Parkinson's Law
> Any task will inflate until all of the available time is spent.

### 23. Peak-End Rule
> People judge an experience largely based on how they felt at its peak and at their end, rather than the total sum or average of every moment of the experience.

### 24. Postel's Law
> Be liberal in what you accept, and conservative in what you send.

### 25. Selective Attention
> The process of focusing our attention only to a subset of stimuli in an environment — usually those related to our goals.

### 26. Serial Position Effect
> Users have a propensity to best remember the first and last items in a series.

### 27. Tesler's Law
> Tesler's Law, also known as The Law of Conservation of Complexity, states that for any system there is a certain amount of complexity which cannot be reduced.

### 28. Von Restorff Effect
> The Von Restorff effect, also known as The Isolation Effect, predicts that when multiple similar objects are present, the one that differs from the rest is most likely to be remembered.

### 29. Zeigarnik Effect
> People remember uncompleted or interrupted tasks better than completed tasks.

Its three takeaways, which are what a design has to satisfy: *"Invite content discovery by providing clear
signifiers of additional content"*; *"Providing artificial progress towards a goal will help to ensure
users are more likely to have the motivation to complete that task"*; *"Provide a clear indication of
progress in order to motivate users to complete tasks."*

## What each law decides in this repository

| Law | The decision it settles |
| --- | --- |
| **Aesthetic-Usability (1)** | Polish never substitutes for a sourced number. The design is quiet because a dense tool should be. |
| **Choice Overload (2)** | Four scenario cards, not a matrix. One recommended action and one alternative, not six buttons. |
| **Chunking (3)** | The stat panel groups five stats and four meta values. The nine database areas are one grid. |
| **Cognitive Bias (4)** | Awareness only, not an applied rule. No anchoring in a preview, no default that flatters a guess, no framing that changes what a number appears to mean. |
| **Cognitive Load (5)** | Progressive disclosure: the card shows three facts, expand for modifiers. |
| **Doherty Threshold (6)** | Every write has a pending label and a saved confirmation. |
| **Fitts (7)** | 44px minimum on everything clickable. The app ships `h-11`. |
| **Flow (8)** | One screen carries the run. No modal stacks over a decision. |
| **Goal-Gradient (9)** | Bars show distance to the target, not only the current value. |
| **Hick (10)** | The option count is bounded on every decision surface. |
| **Jakob (11)** | A conventional sidebar, table, form and stepper. |
| **Common Region (12)** | A card bounds one concept. The Database sub-navigation is one closed region. |
| **Proximity (13)** | A provenance mark sits next to the number it labels. A field label is inside its own `<label>`. |
| **Prägnanz (14)** | One visual idea per card. No ambiguous or dead controls. |
| **Similarity (15)** | One `.btn`, one `.pill`, one `.tag` vocabulary. |
| **Uniform Connectedness (16)** | The inheritance diagram draws the connection between generations. |
| **Mental Model (17)** | A planning desk, not a bot. The app's nouns are the client's nouns. |
| **Miller (18)** | Nav is banded and chunked; the stat panel is five plus four. |
| **Occam (21)** | One save bar, one verb. |
| **Paradox of the Active User (20)** | The Dashboard is usable on first load. No onboarding wall. |
| **Pareto (21)** | The few facts that drive the decision sit first. |
| **Parkinson (22)** | A bounded run with a deadline, not an open-ended dashboard. Nothing fills an empty layout. |
| **Peak-End (23)** | The result screen leads with the outcome and states the honest finding. |
| **Postel (24)** | Accept loose input on import, emit strict canonical labels. |
| **Selective Attention (25)** | The recommendation card is the only accented element. The mood readout is word + arrow + percentage, because colour alone cannot order two tiers at equal luminance. |
| **Serial Position (26)** | Primary action first, summary last. |
| **Tesler (27)** | The engine absorbs the complexity; the player sees a band and a reason line. |
| **Von Restorff (28)** | One `RECOMMENDED` marker, not an accent on every card. |
| **Zeigarnik (29)** | The wizard's four-state step bar: not started, started-unfinished, saved, current. The incomplete state is the one the bar exists to show. |

## The attribution this repository got wrong before

| Claim | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| Continuity and Closure are Laws of UX | They are **Gestalt principles published by uilaws.com**, not by this site. See [the Laws of UI file](uilaws-2026-10-21.md). |
| Common Fate is a published law | **Neither site publishes it.** It is Gestalt, and it is cited from the Gestalt file. |
| There is a "Law of Experience" | **No such law exists** on either site. The concept is **Mental Model**, entry 17 above. |
| "Aesthetic consistency and visual hierarchy" is law 27 | It is a **house principle**, not a law. It is not in this list and is not in the other. |
| The set has thirty laws | This list has **29**. The old count included a non-entry and a house principle. |

## Checkable rules derived

| Id | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| UX-1 | A control is at least 44px, and the primary action is nearest the decision. |
| UX-2 | A progress indicator states distance to the goal, not only the current value. |
| UX-3 | An incomplete state is visible and distinguishable from a saved one. |
| UX-4 | A provenance mark sits beside the number it labels. |
| UX-5 | Two elements that look alike behave alike. |
| UX-6 | The accent appears once per screen, on the thing that matters. |
| UX-7 | The first and last items in a series are the ones that carry the action. |
| UX-8 | Loose input is accepted; the output is a canonical label. |
| UX-9 | A figure never changes meaning by framing. |
| UX-10 | Nothing fills an empty layout. |
